☢ Two ways to keep a reactor cool

A reactor that has been shut down still makes heat — about 6.5% of full power the instant the rods drop, and still ~0.5% a day later. For a 3,400 MW core that is 220 MW falling to 17 MW: enough to boil itself dry and melt in hours if nothing carries that heat away. Every nuclear accident in history is a story about losing that heat path.

Active cooling Gen II — Fukushima, TMI, most of today's fleet

  • Motor-driven pumps push water through the core.
  • Pumps need the grid → then diesels → then batteries.
  • Heat ends up in the sea or a river, via more pumps.
  • Operators must diagnose and act, often in minutes.
  • Chain of dependencies. Break any link and the clock starts.

Passive cooling Gen III+ — AP1000, ESBWR, SMRs

  • Water sits above the core and falls into it.
  • Hot water rises, cold water sinks — natural circulation.
  • Steam condenses on a steel shell cooled by an air draught.
  • Valves open when they lose power (fail-safe).
  • No pump, no diesel, no operator, for 72 hours.

And when is passive not enough? Shake the plant hard enough and the pool above the reactor cracks and drains — try Beyond-Design Quake. It rides that out: the water lands on the containment floor and gravity feeds it straight back in. Only two things actually finish it — losing the containment and turning the passive systems off — and both are in the UI.

How to read the diagram

  • Both buildings are cut open the same way — the near quarter of the wall removed, the roof drawn as glass, the top of each vessel sliced off. Nothing has burst; that is just how you get to look in.
  • Both plants are built the same way. One thing differs: where the spare water sits — in a pool held high beside the reactor on the right, in a basement tank behind a pump on the left.
  • The fuel bundle stands inside the reactor, in the water. Watch the level against the top of it: while the fuel is covered it stays grey; uncovered it goes orange, then red, then slumps.
  • Water you can watch. Slugs of fluid travel the pipes at the speed the model computed; a plain steel pipe has nothing in it. Water heats from blue through a pale scalding colour to orange. If the loop is orange all the way round, nothing is taking the heat away — it is carrying the same heat straight back into the fuel.
  • Under the reactor: how much water is left, how hot, and how much heat the fuel is still making. That last number is the whole problem: shutting a reactor down stops the chain reaction, not the heat.
  • Beside the big pump: what the cooling behind it is made of. Both plants keep creeping round on their own when it stops — the difference is whether what happens next needs pumps.
  • The pipe out of the reactor leaves high and climbs into the boiler; the pipe back leaves the boiler lower and runs down to the pump. Hot water rises and cool water sinks, so that loop keeps turning on its own once the pump stops — but at a crawl, which is what you see when the slugs slow right down.
  • Look into the pump: the impeller and its curved vanes are turning in a casing full of water. When it stops, they stop.
  • Pick a single unit and the turbine hall appears outside the containment. Steam leaves the top of the boiler, spins the turbine, turns the generator — that is where the electricity comes from — and the condenser turns it back into water to be pumped in again. Nothing in that loop has ever been inside the reactor.
  • The line under each plant's name is the step it has reached. Watch them separate.
What is real here, and what is not

Half the value of a simulation is knowing which numbers to trust. So:

ComputedWay–Wigner decay heat. Latent-heat boil-off against the net heat imbalance. Zr–H₂O oxidation above 1100 K with its 6.5 MJ/kg exotherm and 0.044 kg H₂ per kg of zircaloy. Saturated-pool containment pressure, p = pair(T) + psat(T) + pH2. Hydrogen migration and deflagration. Released Cs-137 integrated over time. Every flow animated in the diagram — if dashes are moving, the model is moving that water.
ScaledTime: the AUTO clock compresses quiet hours up to 1800× and drops to near real time when a core is coming apart. The cutaway is an isometric cutaway, not a scale drawing: the same projection as the site view, so the two are one world at two scales, but the machines are sized to be read rather than measured. The layout is the real arrangement of a loop — reactor, hot leg high, boiler, pump on the cold leg low, and back in — and the passive plant's pool really does sit higher than the reactor, because that height is what makes gravity work. What is not real is the spacing: a containment is far emptier than this, and the vessels are drawn far fatter relative to their height than they are. Distance: one map tile is about 25 m, so the whole island is 1.2 km across — the contamination footprint is a schematic of the plume, not a dispersion calculation, and the evacuation rings are indicative rather than measured (a 20 km ring would be four hundred tiles wide). The radius written against each ring in the key is the model's number; the circle drawn for it is not. The site is staged for the camera rather than laid out to a real plant plan.
AssumedBoth units are 3,400 MW thermal so the comparison is like-for-like. The Gen-II unit is a large dry PWR containment; a Mark-I BWR is a harder case. The Gen-III+ unit is AP1000-class: PRHR, CMTs, ADS, IRWST gravity injection and a steel shell cooled by air and an evaporating film. 8 h of battery on the Gen-II unit and 24 h on the Gen-III+, against a 72 h gravity tank. The passive plant is not invulnerable here: past about 1.5× the seismic design basis the in-containment pool cracks and leaks, and past about 2.5× the heat-exchanger line fails as well. Neither on its own melts it down, because the water is still inside the containment and gravity puts it back; what does melt it down is losing the containment, or turning the passive kit off. Try Beyond-Design Quake, Everything At Once and the disable passive systems toggle and watch which one actually breaks it.
FakedHot water is drawn orange. Water at 350 °C is not orange; it is water. The colour is there so you can see where the heat is without reading a number, and it is the one place the picture chooses legibility over literal truth. Three more things are cut or bent for the same reason. The near quarter of the containment wall, and the near half of each vessel head, are cut away, the way a museum model is cut away — nothing has actually opened. The roof is drawn as glass; it is 40 mm of steel. And a pressure vessel runs water-solid, with no free surface at all: the level is drawn with headroom under the head so you can watch it fall, and the percentage beside it is the model's own number. The consequence figures (land, evacuees, cost, latent cancers) and the evacuation rings drawn on the map are scaling laws fitted to two data points — Fukushima ≈15 PBq and Chernobyl ≈85 PBq. They are the right order of magnitude and nothing more. The latent-cancer number is a straight LNT extrapolation, shown because people ask for it, not because the model believes it precisely. The tsunami inundation is cinematic, not hydrodynamic. The condensate loop — water boiled off inside the containment condenses on the cold steel shell and drains back to the pool and the floor — is modelled as closed but not perfect: 6% of what the heat exchanger boils, and 2% of each recirculation pass, is treated as held up and not returned. Those two fractions are a guess, and they are what decides whether the passive unit rides out a total blackout for days or for weeks. With a perfectly closed containment it never runs out; with a leaky one it fails in hours.
